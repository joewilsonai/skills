# Score a video against comparable work

Use this procedure on any subject. Start with the actual audience, format, viewing situation and promise of the episode. A tribute to a coach needs different visual evidence from a lesson in how a computer works. A vertical Short and a chaptered documentary need their own comparisons.

## Research the reference set

1. Write a one-sentence brief with the target viewer, intended experience, runtime and format. Search for the same subject and viewer, then for the same storytelling format. Start with three or four close examples across at least two creators when available. For a countdown, include an actual list or countdown. If close matches are scarce, label each adjacent example with the specific part it can test.
2. Verify **popular** from public reach. Record the creator, upload date, runtime, visible view count, retrieval date and watch URL. Account for upload age, audience size and distribution. If you can check similarly aged uploads in the same format from that creator, report the comparison and its sample. Otherwise describe the observed reach. Include a modest-reach example when it is the closest editorial match. Record why you chose the set before rating your own cut.
3. Verify **good at this part** by inspecting it. Seek to the opening, a substantial middle example, a transition and the payoff. Write the timestamp, what the viewer actually sees or hears, and the job that moment accomplishes. A peer can be excellent at one thing and ordinary at another. A transcript can establish spoken order. Frame samples establish visible content. Audio, pacing and movement need appropriate playback or timed evidence. Check that the transcript, thumbnail and runtime belong to the same public edit.
4. Record all references and observations in the case. Keep the reusable skill free of a permanent favorite-channel list. Give the independent script readers the audience-facing draft first. After their cold retellings are saved, use the reference moments to calibrate scores. This preserves an independent test of whether the story made sense.

## Score the parts the audience experiences

Use **1–5** for each applicable row. **1** means the intended job is missing, misleading or contradicted. **2** means the job is attempted but a newcomer must supply a missing step. **3** means the job works at ordinary viewing pace. **4** means it works and the reviewer can point to a specific improvement in clarity, impact or usefulness over a merely functional treatment. **5** means the moment is exemplary for its job: the viewer can explain the discovery and the reviewer can identify exactly how the evidence, timing, sound or picture made it possible. Use **N/E** for an unassessable part and say which evidence would resolve it.

Rate the relevant **peer moment and ours on the same criterion**. Give both a score, a timestamp or script cue, and a one-sentence reason. Identify the best working reference for that part. If different peers solve the job in different ways, compare the alternatives. Use the numbers as editorial judgments on the named moments. Report the individual findings, not a forecast of views. A weak factual claim or failed newcomer retelling requires a repair regardless of the other scores.

| Part | What to test in ours and the reference |
| --- | --- |
| Title and thumbnail | At feed size, what question or experience does a viewer expect? Does the opening deliver it? |
| Opening | What is the first specific thing a viewer can notice or do? When do they understand why to stay? |
| Each rank | What new discovery does this entry earn? Can a viewer explain it on one listen? Would removing it leave a real gap? |
| Middle and handoffs | Identify the turn, two adjacent chapters it connects, and the change in the audience’s understanding. |
| General-audience clarity | Quote the first unfamiliar term or symbol. Locate the explanation or visible example before it is needed. Retell the point in ordinary language. |
| Evidence and visual explanation | Can viewers see the action, person, original passage or demonstration that earns the claim? Can they distinguish an illustration from a record? |
| Visual clarity at phone size | Play the busiest beat once with captions on. Where did the eye go first? Could the viewer follow the action or read the decisive detail before the cut? Name any competing text and check what deleting it would cost. |
| Series consistency | Compare the thumbnail, opener, middle and conclusion to the Club’s broadcast palette and type roles. Check the same cue in portrait and landscape and distinguish the series treatment from graphics already present in the source. |
| Fit of picture mix | Where does a live scene, original page, still or animation change what the viewer can understand? Compare the time given to that moment and what the viewer can see by its end. |
| Editing and pacing | Time the wait to the first payoff, a demanding explanation and a transition. Can someone finish reading or thinking before the cut? |
| Voice and sound | Listen to the actual exports at ordinary and low volume. Compare delivery, pauses, intelligibility, location sound and the way music enters under speech. |
| Ending | Does the last detail answer the opening question? What could a newcomer now say or do? Does it land before the promotion or credits? |

For a full countdown, use one line per rank for its distinct contribution and one line per major dimension. For a single Short, score its first image, setup, discovery, evidence and exit in the context of other Shorts. Give the most important one to three edits, where each belongs and how to verify it. Keep superseded scores with their cut or script version. Two reviewers can disagree. Show the disputed moment and their reasoning before choosing an edit.

If a reviewer reports hearing a different speaker or language, replay the audio alone at the cited time, then inspect the picture with sound off. An interview may carry translation captions while the film’s narrator is speaking. Record which words were audible, which were visible and whether a viewer could tell who was speaking.

## Measure how the picture is made

Decide what the viewer is mainly being asked to look at in each interval. Use the same definitions on every cut in the comparison. Useful primary categories include a presenter on camera, an interview, filmed action or location footage, archival moving footage, a dramatization or entertainment clip, a still photograph, a facsimile of a document or published figure, a real screen demonstration, explanatory animation or diagram, an authored chart, a text card, and branding or credits. Combine categories in the comparison table when the available evidence only supports the broader distinction. Keep crucial details of provenance, such as a movie clip illustrating an event, in the notes.

- Assign one dominant primary to each interval. If the function changes, split the interval. For a balanced split screen with no clear dominant subject, use a named hybrid consistently. A host explaining a diagram on a lightboard can be presenter-led footage with a secondary diagram. A source page remains a document while its key sentence is highlighted. A scan’s chart can be a source figure, while a newly drawn chart based on its numbers is an authored graphic.
- Track overlays and **actual movement** separately. Subtitles, lower thirds, diagrams beside a presenter, highlights, pans across stills and explanatory reveals can share time with a primary. A photograph with a push-in is still a photograph. A one-second animated build followed by an eight-second hold is nine seconds of a designed graphic and one second of active reveal. Secondary shares can overlap. For sampled images, pair with adjacent frames or play the section before attributing motion.
- **Exact timeline.** For our cut, use the verified export edit decisions when available, split at shot and chapter boundaries, identify the source asset, and check the rendered picture against the log. Use measured in/out times, include the tails and credits, and reconcile the intervals with the final runtime. At dissolves, choose and document a consistent visual handover or use a transition category.
- **Peer sampling.** Full shot logging gives the strongest measurement. For a fast estimate, decide a grid before looking, such as 24 equal-duration bins with one frame at each midpoint. Inspect surrounding frames where movement or source identity matters. Weight the sample by the duration it represents. Record sample count, spacing, unclassifiable samples and the included runtime (including credits or a clearly named content-only cut). Log short shots found during targeted viewing separately from the unbiased grid. Zero observed samples cannot establish that a category never appeared.
- Report **seconds and percent of runtime** for the primary categories, the amount classified, and the separate secondary percentages. Compare the opening, middle and ending when their mixtures change the story. Explain what the material accomplishes before suggesting more or less of it. A peer’s percentage is descriptive. Keep any existing case or series footage target separate and record when its suitability condition applies. A camera shot is worth adding when it shows something the audience otherwise misses.

For repeatable totals, enter intervals in a tab-separated file with `in_s`, `out_s`, `primary`, and optional `also` and `part`. Separate secondary labels with a vertical bar. In sample mode include `at_s`, the observed timestamp within each represented bin. Use `unknown` for an interval you saw but could not classify. For partial review, leave unobserved portions uncovered. Split rows when either the primary or a measured secondary changes.

## Use the evidence at the right stage

At treatment time, choose comparable videos, log their techniques and decide what material the story needs. At script review, score the promise, written sequence, explanations, planned shots and conclusion as **script judgments**. At rough cut, score the actual performance, timing, source visibility, picture mix and sound. When testing a rewrite, keep its predictions separate from the measurements of the earlier export.

After an authorized release, use the channel’s own YouTube Studio analytics, when available, to revisit an editorial hypothesis. For a long video inspect the first 30 seconds and the dips or spikes around named edits. Check the typical range for similar-length videos and consider alternative explanations for a replay or a skip. For packaging, compare traffic sources before interpreting click-through rate. For Shorts, distinguish views from engaged views when comparing releases across the March 2025 counting change. Public peer counts alone cannot identify where competing viewers stayed or why. During an authorized edit, take an actionable finding through the [edit loop](edit-loop.md). Record the baseline and candidate, the viewer’s answer, the keep or revert decision, and the scope in which the winner was verified.
